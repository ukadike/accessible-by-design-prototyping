# CC Fest 2026: Post-Workshop Reflection

**Session:** “Access Is the Interface: Coding in the Age of AI”  
**Event:** Virtual CC Fest, October 17, 2026  
**Recording length:** 52:05  
**Review basis:** Zoom recording transcript and selected screen-share checkpoints, reviewed October 27, 2026.

This is a facilitator reflection on the workshop recording, not a participant survey. Audience responses below are paraphrased and anonymized; the recording transcript may contain transcription errors.

## What worked

- **The p5.js garden made the ideas tangible.** Participants could give an object an intention and description, consider its text alternative and interaction, and see how those decisions could be carried in code and a Schema Card.
- **The session prompted transfer to participants’ own work.** Educators asked how the approach could support their classes and whether AI could help them build learning tools more quickly.
- **The accessibility demonstration led to concrete questions.** Participants asked whether descriptions would be exposed to screen readers and how to keep descriptions concise without losing an artwork’s creative character.
- **The discussion connected accessibility to creative practice.** The closing exchange framed clear description as a starting point, with room for more complex language when the work calls for it.

These responses suggest that the workshop landed as a practical creative-coding and accessibility method. Participants were thinking about applying it beyond the demonstration.

## What needs a clearer explanation

At **22:47**, a participant asked what a Schema Card is. After the explanation, they said the broader context and workshop guide helped. This suggests that the term’s purpose was not yet clear enough at the point participants encountered it.

The Schema Card is the participant’s portable record of an object’s intention, description, text alternative, interaction choices, and accessibility requirements. Its role as the bridge between the playground, the code, and the participant’s take-home artifact should be stated before the first demonstration.

The first five minutes also include arrival and screen-sharing setup. A short arrival activity could make that time useful while giving participants an immediate entry point.

## Changes for the next delivery

1. **Define the Schema Card in the opening.** For example: “A Schema Card records what an interactive object means, how people can access it, and which design decisions the code should carry forward.”
2. **Show the whole sequence early:** choose an object → describe its intention → set its access rules → inspect the resulting code → export the Schema Card.
3. **Give early arrivals a small prompt** and begin the main walkthrough at a stated time.
4. **Zoom in on the playground during screen share** and reduce browser chrome so instructions and controls are easier to read.
5. **Close with a short recap:** what participants made, what the Schema Card preserves, and where to find the workshop guide and code.

## Signals to look for next time

- Participants can explain the Schema Card in their own words before the demo ends.
- Participants can create or export a card without needing the facilitator to restate the workflow.
- Screen-reader users can confirm that the object descriptions and interaction information are available to assistive technology.
- Educators can name one way they might adapt the exercise for their own class or creative practice.
